Kyoto, then called Heian-kyo, was Japan's imperial capital from 794 to 1869.
Kyoto's grid of streets was modelled on Chang'an (today's Xi'an), the capital of Tang-dynasty China.
Kyoto simply means "capital city".
Kyoto has around 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines.
Kyoto's UNESCO listing covers 17 sites spread across Kyoto, Uji and Otsu.
Kyoto was taken off the atomic bomb target list in 1945, largely because US Secretary of War Henry Stimson knew and valued the city.
The Onin War (1467–1477) destroyed much of Kyoto. Locals joke that when they say "the last war", they mean that one.
Fushimi Inari Taisha is famous for its thousands of vermilion torii gates, often said to number around 10,000.
Each torii at Fushimi Inari is donated by a company or person, and the donor's name and date are written on the back.
A large torii at Fushimi Inari can cost over a million yen to donate.
Fushimi Inari is the head shrine of about 30,000 Inari shrines across Japan.
Inari is the god of rice and business, which is why so many companies donate gates.
Foxes are Inari's messengers. Many fox statues hold a key in their mouth, said to be the key to the rice granary.
Inari sushi (rice in fried tofu pouches) is named after Inari, because foxes are said to love fried tofu.
Fushimi Inari dates back to 711.
Fushimi Inari never closes and is free, so you can walk the gates early in the morning or at night.
Hiking to the top of Mt Inari (233 m) and back takes about two to three hours.
Kiyomizu-dera's famous wooden stage stands about 13 m above the slope on a grid of huge pillars, built without nails.
"Jumping off the stage at Kiyomizu" is a Japanese saying for taking a bold leap. In the Edo period over 200 people actually jumped, and most survived.
Kiyomizu means "pure water". It's named after the Otowa Waterfall below the main hall.
The Otowa Waterfall has three streams, said to bring long life, love and success in study. Drinking from all three is considered greedy.
Kiyomizu-dera was founded in 778.
Kiyomizu-dera holds special night illuminations for the autumn leaves.
Jishu Shrine at Kiyomizu-dera has two "love stones". Walk from one to the other with your eyes closed and you'll find love.
Legend says if you trip on Sannenzaka slope near Kiyomizu-dera, you'll have three years of bad luck. Shops sell gourd charms to protect you.
There's a Starbucks on Ninenzaka in a 100-year-old townhouse with tatami seating.
The Yasaka Pagoda in Higashiyama is a five-storey pagoda about 46 m tall, rebuilt in 1440.
Yasaka Shrine in Gion is the head of the Gion shrines and runs the Gion Matsuri.
The Gion Matsuri in July is over 1,100 years old. It began in 869 to calm the gods believed to cause plague.
In Kyoto, geisha are called geiko, and apprentices are called maiko.
Some private alleys in Gion are now closed to tourists, and there are fines for trespassing.
Kinkaku-ji's top two floors are covered in gold leaf. The original pavilion burned down in 1950 and was rebuilt in 1955.
The Silver Pavilion (Ginkaku-ji) was never covered in silver.
Ginkaku-ji's cone of sand, Kogetsudai, is said to be for moon viewing.
Ryoan-ji's famous rock garden has 15 stones, but from any spot on the veranda you can only see 14 at once.
The Philosopher's Path is named after philosopher Nishida Kitaro, who walked it every day.
Sanjusangendo houses 1,001 life-size statues of Kannon in a wooden hall 120 m long.
Toji's five-storey pagoda is Japan's tallest wooden pagoda, at about 55 m.
A big flea market fills the grounds of Toji on the 21st of every month.
Nijo Castle has "nightingale floors" that chirp when you walk on them, a built-in alarm against intruders.
At Nijo Castle in 1867, the last shogun announced that he was handing power back to the emperor.
Higashi Honganji's Founder's Hall is one of the largest wooden buildings in the world.
To build Higashi Honganji in the 1880s, women donated their hair to make super-strong ropes for hauling timber. One rope is still on display.
Heian Shrine was built in 1895 for the 1,100th anniversary of the capital. Its giant torii is 24 m tall.
Kitano Tenmangu enshrines the scholar Sugawara no Michizane and is packed with students praying before exams.
Saiho-ji, the "Moss Temple", has over 100 kinds of moss, and you can only visit with a reservation.
Late November is peak autumn-leaf season in Kyoto, and places like Eikando and Tofuku-ji get very busy.
Eikando's main Buddha statue is unusual: it looks back over its shoulder.
Tofuku-ji's Tsutenkyo Bridge looks out over a valley full of maple trees and is one of Kyoto's most famous autumn views.
In Arashiyama, Togetsukyo means "Moon Crossing Bridge". An emperor named it after the moon seemed to cross over it.
The sound of wind in Arashiyama's bamboo grove is one of Japan's official "100 Soundscapes".
Some bamboo species can grow nearly a metre in a single day.
Tenryu-ji in Arashiyama, founded in 1339, ranks first among Kyoto's Five Great Zen temples.
Tenryu-ji's pond garden was designed by Zen master Muso Soseki and still keeps much of its 14th-century layout.
At Iwatayama Monkey Park in Arashiyama, the humans go inside a cage to feed the monkeys.
The Okochi Sanso villa in Arashiyama belonged to silent-film star Denjiro Okochi, who spent 30 years building its gardens.
The Hozugawa river boat ride runs about two hours down the gorge from Kameoka to Arashiyama.
Yudofu, gently simmered tofu, is a Kyoto temple dish, especially popular around Arashiyama and Nanzen-ji.
Kyoto has some of the highest bread spending per household of any city in Japan.
Nintendo was founded in Kyoto in 1889 as a maker of hanafuda playing cards.
Kyocera, Murata, Omron, Shimadzu and Wacoal are all headquartered in Kyoto.
Being offered "bubuzuke" (rice with tea poured over it) in a Kyoto home is a famous polite hint that it's time to go home.
Kyoto addresses often use "agaru" (go north) and "sagaru" (go south) from the nearest crossroads.
A traditional children's song, "Maru Take Ebisu", lists Kyoto's east-west streets in order so people can remember them.
Old Kyoto townhouses (machiya) are called "eel beds" because they are narrow and deep. Taxes were once based on frontage width.
Nishiki Market, "Kyoto's Kitchen", is a narrow covered street about 400 m long with over 100 shops.
Pontocho alley's name may come from the Portuguese word "ponto", meaning point.
Couples sitting along the Kamo River tend to space themselves at almost equal distances, a habit locals call the "Kamogawa equal-spacing rule".
Turtle-shaped stepping stones cross the Kamo River at Demachiyanagi.
Shijo-Karasuma, near your hotel, is Kyoto's main business and banking district.
Kyoto's townscape rules limit building heights and colours, so even McDonald's and convenience-store signs are toned down.
Kyoto Tower, 131 m tall, opened in 1964 and is the tallest structure in the city.
Kyoto Station's huge glass building (1997) has a 171-step "Grand Stairway" that lights up with LED displays at night.
The Kyoto Imperial Palace was the emperor's home until 1869, and the Taisho and Showa emperors were enthroned there.
Kyoto's Gozan Okuribi in August lights giant bonfire characters on the mountains around the city.
Kyoto's three big festivals are Aoi Matsuri (May), Gion Matsuri (July) and Jidai Matsuri (October).
The Kyoto Protocol climate treaty was agreed in Kyoto in 1997.
Kyoto University's Hideki Yukawa became Japan's first Nobel Prize winner in 1949.
The Urasenke and Omotesenke tea ceremony schools are both based in Kyoto.
Ichiwa, a shop near Imamiya Shrine, has sold grilled rice cakes since around the year 1000, making it one of the oldest businesses in the world.
Yatsuhashi, cinnamon-flavoured rice-flour sweets, is Kyoto's classic souvenir.
Saba-zushi (mackerel sushi) became a Kyoto speciality thanks to the "Mackerel Road" that brought salted fish from the coast.
Kyo-yasai are Kyoto's heirloom vegetables, like round Kamo eggplant and Kujo green onions.
Nishijin in Kyoto has been famous for silk weaving for over 500 years.
The Kyoto International Manga Museum is in a former elementary school and holds about 300,000 items.
Japan's first electric streetcar ran in Kyoto in 1895.
Kyoto is the setting of the novel and film Memoirs of a Geisha.
Kyoto's Kamo River is so clean that you can sometimes see herons and ducks fishing in the middle of the city.
Arashiyama's Sagano Romantic Train is an old-style sightseeing train along the Hozugawa gorge.
Kyoto's machiya often have narrow inner gardens called tsuboniwa to let in light and air.
Matcha sweets are everywhere in Kyoto because Uji, Japan's most famous tea town, is right next door.
