Nara, then called Heijo-kyo, was Japan's capital from 710 to 784.
Japan's oldest surviving histories, the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720), were written during the Nara period.
The Man'yoshu, Japan's oldest poetry collection, comes from the Nara period. The current era name "Reiwa" was taken from it.
Nara's UNESCO listing, "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara", covers eight sites.
About 1,300 wild sika deer roam Nara Park.
According to legend, the god of Kasuga Taisha arrived in Nara riding a white deer, so deer are seen as sacred messengers.
Nara's deer are a protected Natural Monument. Historically, killing one could be punished by death.
Nara's deer are wild. Nobody owns them, but they're used to people.
Many Nara deer have learned to bow for shika senbei, special deer crackers.
Shika senbei are made from wheat flour and rice bran, with no sugar, so they're safe for deer.
A deer's "bow" can also be a warning before a headbutt. Show empty hands when you've run out of crackers.
Autumn is rutting season, so male deer can be more pushy in October and November.
Every October, the male deer's antlers are cut off in a ceremony that has been held since the 1600s.
Plastic bags are dangerous for Nara's deer because they sometimes eat them. Keep your rubbish in your bag.
Nara's deer are often seen waiting at pedestrian crossings.
Nara Park covers about 660 hectares, including the temple and shrine grounds.
Todai-ji means "Great Eastern Temple".
Todai-ji's Great Buddha is about 15 m tall and one of the largest bronze Buddhas in the world.
Making the Great Buddha in the 8th century used up a huge share of Japan's copper.
The Great Buddha was opened in 752 when an Indian monk painted in its eyes, holding a brush tied to long cords that the crowd also held.
The Great Buddha has been damaged by fire and earthquakes. Its current head dates from the 1690s.
Todai-ji's Great Buddha Hall is one of the largest wooden buildings in the world, even though today's 1709 hall is about a third smaller than the original.
A pillar in the Great Buddha Hall has a hole the same size as the Buddha's nostril. Crawling through it is said to bring enlightenment.
The two 8 m wooden guardians in Todai-ji's Nandaimon gate were carved by Unkei and Kaikei's team in about 70 days in 1203.
Todai-ji's Omizutori fire ritual in March has been held every year since 752 without a break.
Todai-ji's Nigatsu-do hall has a free wooden veranda with a wide view over Nara.
The monk Gyoki raised money from ordinary people to build the Great Buddha. His statue stands outside Kintetsu Nara Station.
Shosoin, Todai-ji's treasure house, holds 8th-century objects, including glass and instruments that came along the Silk Road.
Some Shosoin treasures go on show at the Nara National Museum each autumn.
Kasuga Taisha was founded in 768 by the powerful Fujiwara clan.
Kasuga Taisha has about 3,000 lanterns, and they're all lit only twice a year, in February and August.
The Kasugayama Primeval Forest behind the shrine has been protected from logging since 841.
Kofuku-ji was the Fujiwara family temple.
Kofuku-ji's five-storey pagoda, about 50 m tall, is Japan's second-tallest wooden pagoda. It is under major repair until the early 2030s.
Kofuku-ji's Ashura statue, from 734, has three faces and six arms and is one of Japan's best-loved Buddhist statues.
Horyu-ji, near Nara, has the world's oldest surviving wooden buildings, from around the 7th century.
Some roof tiles at Gango-ji in Nara date from the late 6th century and are among the oldest still in use in Japan.
Naramachi, Nara's old merchant quarter, has red cloth monkey charms (migawari-zaru) hanging under the eaves to keep bad luck away.
Wakakusayama, the grassy hill in Nara Park, is set on fire every January in the Yamayaki festival.
Isuien garden uses "borrowed scenery": Todai-ji's gate and the hills behind it look like part of the garden.
The reconstructed Suzaku Gate and Great Hall at the Heijo Palace site show how big Nara's old palace was.
Nakatanidou, a mochi shop in Nara, is famous for super-fast mochi pounding.
Nara's mascot Sento-kun, a boy Buddha with deer antlers, was controversial at first but became popular.
Kakinoha-zushi, sushi wrapped in persimmon leaves, is a Nara speciality.
Nara is one of Japan's top persimmon producers.
Narazuke are vegetables pickled in sake lees, a Nara speciality.
Miwa somen, thin wheat noodles from Sakurai in Nara, are said to be the origin of somen in Japan.
Shoryaku-ji in Nara is said to be where important sake brewing methods were developed in the Middle Ages.
Nara makes about 90% of Japan's solid ink sticks (sumi) for calligraphy.
Yamatokoriyama in Nara is Japan's goldfish capital and hosts a national goldfish-scooping championship.
Yoshino in Nara has about 30,000 cherry trees on its mountain slopes.
Yoshino kudzu starch is used in fine Japanese sweets.
The Nara Hotel, opened in 1909, has hosted guests including Albert Einstein and Audrey Hepburn.
Asuka, south of Nara, has Ishibutai, a huge stone tomb from the 7th century with its earth mound gone.
The tomb of Emperor Jimmu, Japan's legendary first emperor, is in Kashihara, Nara.
Nara is about 45 minutes from Kyoto by Kintetsu train.
Park rangers sometimes call the deer by blowing a horn in events called "shika-yose", and herds come running.
Nara's deer are counted every year, and their numbers have grown in recent years.
Deer-crossing road signs are everywhere in Nara Park.
Some Nara deer like to nap right in the middle of temple paths.
