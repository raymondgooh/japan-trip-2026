Mt Fuji is 3,776 m tall, Japan's highest mountain.
Mt Fuji is an active volcano. It last erupted in 1707, dropping ash on Edo (Tokyo) about 100 km away.
The 1707 Hoei eruption began just 49 days after one of Japan's biggest earthquakes.
Fuji's near-perfect cone is really three volcanoes stacked on top of each other: Komitake, Ko-Fuji ("Old Fuji") and Shin-Fuji ("New Fuji").
Fuji's lava is basalt, which is unusual for a cone volcano. It flows more easily, which helps give Fuji its smooth slopes.
The base of Mt Fuji is roughly 40–50 km across.
Mt Fuji became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013 as a cultural site, a "sacred place and source of artistic inspiration", not as a natural one.
In Fujisan, "san" means mountain. It isn't the polite "-san" used with people's names.
Japanese people rarely say "Fujiyama". It's Fujisan.
Mt Fuji sits on the border of Yamanashi and Shizuoka prefectures, and the border near the summit has never been officially drawn.
Everything above the 8th station of Mt Fuji is officially owned by the Fujisan Hongu Sengen Taisha shrine.
Fuji is sacred in Shinto and is home to the goddess Konohanasakuya-hime.
Legend says the first person to climb Fuji was the mountain ascetic En no Gyoja, around 663.
Women were banned from climbing Mt Fuji until 1872.
The climbing season is only July to early September. Outside it, the trails are closed and dangerous.
About 200,000 people climb Mt Fuji each summer.
Since 2024, climbers on the popular Yoshida Trail must pay a fee, and there's a daily limit on numbers.
A Japanese saying goes: "A wise man climbs Fuji once; only a fool climbs it twice."
Watching sunrise from Fuji's summit is called "goraiko", the arrival of the light.
A summer-only post office operates at the summit of Mt Fuji.
Water boils at about 87 °C on Fuji's summit because of the low air pressure.
The average yearly temperature on Fuji's summit is around −6 °C.
Fuji has four main climbing trails: Yoshida, Subashiri, Gotemba and Fujinomiya.
Walking around the summit crater is called "ohachi-meguri", circling the bowl.
The highest point, Kengamine, is on the rim of the summit crater.
A weather radar dome sat on Fuji's summit until 1999. It's now in a museum in Fujiyoshida.
Mt Fuji's first snowcap usually appears in early autumn, but in 2024 it didn't come until 7 November, the latest on record.
Fuji is most often visible in the cold, dry months from late autumn to winter, and early mornings are usually clearest.
A cap-shaped cloud (kasagumo) sitting on Fuji's peak is a local sign that rain is on the way.
"Diamond Fuji" is when the sun rises or sets exactly on the summit, like a sparkling jewel.
"Sakasa Fuji" (upside-down Fuji) is the mirror image of the mountain on a still lake, best on calm early mornings.
On very clear days, Mt Fuji can be seen from over 300 km away. The record is from Wakayama.
Hokusai's "Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji", which includes The Great Wave, actually has 46 prints.
Hokusai's Great Wave, with Fuji in the background, is printed on the back of the new 1,000-yen note issued in 2024.
The view of Fuji from Lake Motosu is printed on the back of the older 1,000-yen note.
Japanese passports issued since 2020 have Hokusai's Fuji prints on their visa pages.
23 February is "Fuji Day", because 2-2-3 can be read "fu-ji-san".
Some Fuji worshippers in Edo built mini Fujis (Fujizuka) in Tokyo for people who couldn't make the trip.
Kawaguchiko is one of the Fuji Five Lakes: Kawaguchi, Yamanaka, Sai, Shoji and Motosu.
The Fuji Five Lakes were formed when lava flows from Mt Fuji dammed rivers.
Lakes Sai and Shoji were once part of one big lake, which lava from the 864 Jogan eruption split apart.
Lake Yamanaka is the largest and highest of the five lakes, at about 980 m.
Lake Motosu is the deepest of the five lakes, at about 120 m.
Lake Shoji is the smallest of the Fuji Five Lakes.
Lake Kawaguchi is the second-largest of the five lakes and the most developed for visitors.
Unoshima in Lake Kawaguchi is the only island in any of the Fuji Five Lakes.
The town you're staying in is officially called Fujikawaguchiko.
In 2024, Fujikawaguchiko town put up a big screen near a Lawson shop to stop crowds photographing Fuji "on top of" the convenience store.
The Kachi Kachi Ropeway is named after "Kachi-kachi Yama", a folk tale about a clever rabbit and a mischievous tanuki.
Kawaguchiko's Momiji Corridor, a tunnel of maple trees on the north shore, is lit up at night during the autumn leaves festival in November.
Oishi Park's kochia bushes on the lake shore turn bright red in autumn.
Chureito Pagoda, the five-storey pagoda in the classic Fuji photo, was built in 1963 as a peace memorial.
Climbing to the Chureito Pagoda viewpoint takes about 400 steps.
Fujiyoshida's Kitaguchi Hongu Fuji Sengen Shrine is the traditional starting point for pilgrims climbing Fuji.
Fujiyoshida's huge Fire Festival in late August marks the end of the climbing season.
The Fuji Excursion limited express has run straight from Shinjuku to Kawaguchiko since 2019.
The Fuji Subaru Line road climbs to Fuji's 5th station at 2,305 m.
Narusawa Ice Cave, formed by lava, stays below freezing all year.
The Fugaku Wind Cave near Lake Sai was used as a natural fridge for storing silkworm eggs.
At the Funatsu lava tree molds, flowing lava swallowed tree trunks and left hollow tubes behind.
Oshino Hakkai, near Kawaguchiko, is a cluster of eight ponds fed by snowmelt that has filtered through Fuji's lava for years.
Yamanashi produces a large share of Japan's bottled mineral water, much of it from Fuji's underground springs.
Yamanashi is Japan's top producer of grapes and peaches, and it makes a lot of Japan's wine, including the local Koshu grape.
Hoto is Yamanashi's soul food: thick, flat noodles stewed in miso broth with pumpkin and vegetables.
Legend says warlord Takeda Shingen fed hoto to his soldiers, cutting the noodles with his sword.
Yoshida udon from Fujiyoshida is famously firm and chewy, traditionally topped with cabbage.
Shingen mochi, a Yamanashi sweet, comes as rice cakes in roasted soybean flour that you pour brown sugar syrup over.
In winter, anglers on Lake Yamanaka fish for wakasagi smelt from heated, domed boats.
Swans live on Lake Yamanaka, and the tourist boats there are shaped like swans too.
Fuji-Q Highland near Kawaguchiko is famous for extreme roller coasters and a huge haunted hospital walk-through.
The Kawaguchiko Music Forest Museum is full of antique music boxes and huge dance organs.
The Itchiku Kubota Art Museum in Kawaguchiko shows kimono that are each dyed as part of a landscape.
Mt Fuji appears in poems in the Man'yoshu, Japan's oldest poetry collection, from the 8th century.
The Mishima Skywalk is Japan's longest pedestrian suspension bridge, at 400 m, with Fuji views.
Mishima's clear Genbe River is fed by springs of Fuji snowmelt that bubble up in the middle of town.
Mishima is famous for eel. Locals say the Fuji spring water used to keep the eels cleans out any muddy taste.
Kakitagawa Springs near Mishima is one of the biggest springs in Japan, gushing about a million tonnes of water a day.
On the Shinkansen from Tokyo to Kyoto, Mt Fuji appears on the right-hand side (seats D and E).
Hikers say Mt Fuji looks very different up close: a dark, rocky slope of volcanic gravel rather than a smooth white cone.
Many sento bathhouses around Japan have a Mt Fuji mural on the wall, so you can "bathe with Fuji".
