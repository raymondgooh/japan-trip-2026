Tokyo was called Edo until 1868, when it was renamed Tokyo, meaning "Eastern Capital".
Kyoto was the imperial capital for over 1,000 years before the emperor moved to Tokyo in 1869.
Greater Tokyo is the most populous metropolitan area in the world, with around 37 million people.
Shinjuku Station is the busiest railway station in the world, with over 3 million passengers a day on average.
Shinjuku Station has more than 200 exits, so it's easy to get lost. Pick your exit before you arrive.
The Shibuya Scramble Crossing can see up to around 3,000 people crossing at once at peak times.
Hachiko, the dog statue at Shibuya, honours an Akita who waited at the station every day for about nine years after his owner died in 1925.
The Hachiko statue you see today is the second one. The original was melted down for metal during World War II.
Shibuya Sky, on top of Shibuya Scramble Square, has an open-air rooftop about 230 m above the crossing.
Shibuya's scramble crossing appears in films like Lost in Translation and The Fast and the Furious: Tokyo Drift.
Shibuya 109 opened in 1979 and became the centre of Japan's "gyaru" fashion scene.
Miyashita Park in Shibuya is a public park built on the roof of a shopping mall.
Senso-ji in Asakusa is Tokyo's oldest temple, traditionally founded in 628.
Legend says two fishermen brothers pulled a small golden statue of Kannon out of the Sumida River, which led to Senso-ji's founding.
The giant red lantern hanging in Asakusa's Kaminarimon gate weighs about 700 kg.
Kaminarimon means "Thunder Gate". It is guarded by statues of Fujin, god of wind, and Raijin, god of thunder.
Nakamise-dori, the 250 m shopping street leading to Senso-ji, is one of the oldest shopping streets in Japan.
Senso-ji's main hall was destroyed in the 1945 air raids and rebuilt in concrete in 1958.
Hanayashiki in Asakusa, opened in 1853, is considered Japan's oldest amusement park.
Asakusa's Sanja Matsuri in May is one of Tokyo's wildest festivals, drawing around 2 million people.
Tokyo Skytree is 634 m tall. 6-3-4 can be read "mu-sa-shi", the old name of the region.
Tokyo Skytree is the tallest tower in the world and the second-tallest structure after the Burj Khalifa.
Tokyo Tower (333 m) was inspired by the Eiffel Tower and is painted white and "international orange" to meet aviation safety rules.
Meiji Jingu near Harajuku is dedicated to Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, who died in 1912 and 1914.
Meiji Jingu's forest of about 100,000 trees was donated from all over Japan and planted by hand around a century ago.
The forest at Meiji Jingu was designed to look after itself. Fallen leaves are swept back under the trees instead of being removed.
Meiji Jingu's great torii gate is made of Taiwanese cypress said to be over 1,500 years old.
Meiji Jingu usually gets more visitors than any other shrine in Japan for the New Year's first visit, often over 3 million.
Takeshita Street in Harajuku became famous for crepes after the first crepe stands opened there in the late 1970s.
Harajuku Station's old wooden building from 1924 was one of Tokyo's oldest wooden stations until it was replaced in 2020.
Yoyogi Park was the site of Japan's first powered aeroplane flight in 1910.
Yoyogi Park was the athletes' village for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics.
The Yoyogi National Gymnasium, with its swooping roof by architect Kenzo Tange, was built for the 1964 Olympic swimming events.
Ueno Zoo, opened in 1882, is Japan's oldest zoo.
Ueno Park was one of Japan's first public parks, designated in 1873.
The statue of Saigo Takamori in Ueno Park shows him in casual clothes walking his dog. His wife reportedly said it didn't look like him.
Ameyoko market near Ueno began as a black market after World War II. The name is linked to "ame" (candy), and some say to "America".
The Tokyo National Museum in Ueno, founded in 1872, is Japan's oldest museum.
Yanaka, near Ueno, survived both the 1923 earthquake and the wartime bombing, so it keeps the feel of old Tokyo.
Ginza is named after a silver coin mint set up there in 1612 ("gin" means silver, "za" means guild).
Ginza's main street, Chuo-dori, closes to cars on weekend afternoons and becomes a "pedestrian paradise".
The Wako clock tower at the Ginza 4-chome crossing is one of Tokyo's most famous landmarks. The current building dates from 1932.
Japan's first McDonald's opened in Ginza in 1971.
The first Starbucks outside North America opened in Ginza in 1996.
Ginza had Japan's first gas street lamps, lit in 1874.
Tokyo Station's red-brick Marunouchi building opened in 1914, and its domes were restored to the original design in 2012.
More than 3,000 trains pass through Tokyo Station every day.
The Imperial Palace stands on the site of Edo Castle, the shogun's former seat.
In the late-1980s bubble, the Imperial Palace grounds were said to be worth more than all the real estate in California.
Nihonbashi bridge was the starting point of Japan's five great Edo-era highways, and road distances are still measured from a marker there.
Mitsukoshi began in 1673 as a kimono shop called Echigoya and is Japan's oldest department store chain.
Shimokitazawa is Tokyo's vintage-clothing and indie-music neighbourhood, packed with small live houses and theatres.
In Shimokitazawa, the Odakyu line moved underground in 2013, and the old track bed became a long strip of shops and green space.
Akabane, where you're staying, is famous for "senbero" bars, places where you can get tipsy for about 1,000 yen.
Akabane is in Kita ward, on the northern edge of Tokyo, just across the Arakawa River from Saitama.
Kabukicho in Shinjuku is named after a kabuki theatre that was planned there after World War II but never built.
A giant Godzilla head peers over Kabukicho from the Hotel Gracery Shinjuku, and it roars at set times.
Omoide Yokocho ("Memory Lane") in Shinjuku grew out of a late-1940s black market and is lined with tiny yakitori stalls.
Golden Gai in Shinjuku squeezes about 200 tiny bars into six narrow alleys. Some seat only five people.
The Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building in Shinjuku has free observation decks 202 m up. On clear days you can see Mt Fuji.
Shinjuku Gyoen was once a feudal lord's estate and mixes French, English and Japanese garden styles.
Tokyo has more Michelin-starred restaurants than any other city in the world.
Nigiri sushi was born in Edo in the 1820s as fast food sold from street stalls.
Monjayaki, a runny cousin of okonomiyaki, is a Tokyo speciality, especially in Tsukishima.
Tokyo's inner fish market moved from Tsukiji to Toyosu in 2018, but Tsukiji's outer market of food stalls is still going.
A single bluefin tuna sold for 333.6 million yen at the 2019 New Year auction in Toyosu.
Kappabashi is the street where restaurants buy kitchenware and the famous plastic food samples.
The yellow ginkgo leaf is the symbol of the Tokyo Metropolitan Government.
Late November is peak season for Tokyo's ginkgo trees, which turn bright yellow.
The ginkgo avenue at Jingu Gaien has 146 trees, planted in 1923 and pruned into neat cone shapes.
Tokyo Banana, a banana-cream sponge cake launched in 1991, is one of Tokyo's best-selling souvenirs.
Tokyo's tap water is safe to drink, and the city even bottles and sells it as "Tokyo Water".
Japanese train companies hand out "delay certificates" so passengers can prove to their boss that the train was late, sometimes for just five minutes.
Station staff called "oshiya" used to be employed to push passengers into packed rush-hour trains.
The Yamanote Line is a loop of 30 stations and takes about an hour to go all the way round.
Many Yamanote Line stations play their own departure jingle. Takadanobaba plays the Astro Boy theme.
Ebisu Station plays the Yebisu Beer theme tune. The district was named after the beer brewery, not the other way round.
Japan's first railway opened in 1872 between Shimbashi in Tokyo and Yokohama.
The Shinkansen bullet train opened in 1964, just before the Tokyo Olympics.
Tokyo hosted the Summer Olympics in 1964 and again in 2021 (the "2020" Games, postponed a year).
Tokyo was largely destroyed twice in the 20th century: by the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake and by the 1945 air raids.
Tokyo has 23 special wards, each run almost like its own city.
Tokyo Metropolis also includes the Ogasawara Islands, about 1,000 km south in the Pacific.
Odaiba's name comes from cannon batteries ("daiba") built in Tokyo Bay in 1853 to defend against Commodore Perry's Black Ships.
Akihabara's electronics district grew out of a post-war black market for radio parts.
The emoji was invented in Japan in 1999 by Shigetaka Kurita for NTT Docomo phones.
Gotokuji temple in Setagaya claims to be the birthplace of the maneki-neko beckoning cat and is filled with thousands of them.
Japan has around 4 million vending machines, about one for every 30 people.
In Tokyo, people stand on the left of escalators and walk on the right. In Osaka it's the other way round.
Most Tokyo taxis have doors that the driver opens and closes for you, so don't slam them.
Tipping isn't expected in Tokyo and can cause confusion. Staff may chase you to return the money.
Tokyo police receive millions of lost items each year, and billions of yen in lost cash are handed in.
Many Tokyo sento (public bathhouses) have a giant Mt Fuji mural, a tradition that started in 1912.
The official cherry tree that signals the start of Tokyo's blossom season is at Yasukuni Shrine.
Tokyo Dome, home of the Yomiuri Giants, is nicknamed "the Big Egg".
Edo was once one of the largest cities in the world, with around a million people in the 1700s.
The first Japanese convenience store chains started in the 1970s. Tokyo now has thousands of konbini, many open 24 hours.
Tokyo's address system numbers city blocks rather than streets, and most streets have no names.
Tokyo's Kabuki-za theatre in Ginza first opened in 1889. The current building, from 2013, has a skyscraper rising behind it.
Haneda Airport was Tokyo's main international airport until Narita opened in 1978, and it took back many international flights from 2010.
